# Asset-Class Normalization Rules

Apply these in Step 2 (Rebuild True NOI) per asset class. Where a class isn't covered,
apply general institutional principles, flag the deviation, and ask the user for
market-specific norms.

> **Which NOI is on screen.** The shipped workbook reports **NOI on a lender basis —
> before replacement reserves** — and **owner cash flow after reserves**. Both numbers
> are correct; they answer different questions. When you quote NOI, say which basis
> you're on, so the user never mistakes a lender-basis NOI for money they can spend.

## FIRST QUESTION ON ANY OPERATING ASSET — is the real estate in the deal?

*** ASK THIS BEFORE ANYTHING ELSE, AND SAY THE ANSWER OUT LOUD. *** A car wash, a motel,
a marina, a laundromat, an assisted living facility and a restaurant are each sold three
different ways, and the three underwrite nothing alike. Getting this wrong is not a
rounding error — it decides what you are buying and how it is financed.

| What is being sold | What you are buying | How it is valued |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Business only** | The operation — equipment, staff, customers, name, cash flow. Someone else owns the building. | A multiple of owner earnings. Your rent becomes an ongoing expense, and the lease you inherit IS the deal. |
| **Business + real estate** | Both, in one price. | Two values added together. They must be separated before either is trusted. |
| **Real estate only** | The building, with an operator as your tenant. | The lease. This is the net-lease case above. |

### Separating the two — the market-rent test

One rule does the whole job. **Charge the business a market rent for the space it
occupies.** What remains after that rent is business profit.

- **Value the real estate** by capitalising that market rent, exactly as you would any
  other income property.
- **Value the business** on a multiple of what is left after the rent.
- **Add them.** If the asking price exceeds the sum, you are overpaying for one side or
  the other, and you must say which.

Run this even when the seller presents one blended number, because a blended number hides
the most common mispricing in small commercial: **rent counted as business profit.** An
owner-operator who pays himself no rent shows inflated earnings, and a multiple applied to
those earnings prices the building twice — once in the multiple, once in the price.

**Related-party or below-market rent is the same trap wearing a lease.** When the seller
owns both sides, the rent between them is whatever they chose for tax reasons. Reset it to
market before valuing anything, and expect a real third-party rent after closing.

### What else changes when a business is attached

- **The price must be allocated in the purchase agreement** — real property, equipment,
  inventory, non-compete, goodwill. It drives depreciation, how goodwill is treated, and
  often the transfer tax. It is negotiated, not discovered later, and buyer and seller
  usually want opposite allocations. This is a CPA and attorney question; flag it, name the
  line items, and never assert the tax outcome.
- **Financing splits too.** Real estate and business assets carry different terms,
  different amortisation and different lender appetite; an SBA-eligible operating business
  is a different conversation from a commercial mortgage. Model them separately or the
  debt service is wrong.
- **Earnings are owner-dependent.** Ask what the owner does daily and what replacing them
  costs. Add a real manager's salary to expenses before applying any multiple — on a
  small operation that single adjustment routinely moves value by a third.
- **Licences, permits and franchise agreements may not transfer**, and some require the
  buyer to qualify personally. A licence that does not run with the land can end the
  income on closing day.
- **Employees, and whether they stay.** In a business sale the staff are the operation.
  Key-person departure is an income risk, not an HR footnote.
- **Inventory and working capital are separate from the purchase price** in most business
  sales, and they are real cash at close that no cap rate will show you.

*** IF THE BUSINESS AND THE DIRT CANNOT BE SEPARATED FROM WHAT YOU HAVE, SAY SO AND STOP
SHORT OF A VALUE. *** Give the real estate figure, state plainly what is missing to value
the business, and list the documents needed: three years of tax returns, a profit and loss
with owner add-backs itemised, the equipment schedule, the lease, and the licences.

## Multifamily (5+ units)

- Vacancy: greater of actual trailing-12 or 5% (submarket-specific if known).
- Management: 8–10% of EGI for <50 units even if owner-managed (your time isn't free).
- Repairs & maintenance: $500–800/unit/yr minimum; age-adjust upward.
- CapEx reserve: $250–350/unit/yr, separate from R&M — outside the NOI presented to
  a lender, always inside YOUR cash flow. (This is the lender-basis vs. owner-basis
  split noted at the top: the workbook's NOI line is before reserves, the owner cash
  flow line is after.)
- Payroll appears at ~60+ units; don't believe zero-payroll T-12s above that.
- Watch: RUBS income durability, concessions netted out of GPR, month-to-month share.

## RV parks / campgrounds

- Separate income stems: lot rent (durable) vs. nightly/transient (seasonal, volatile)
  vs. ancillary (store, laundry — margin, not revenue). Cap-rate each stem's risk.
- Seasonality: normalize on trailing-24 months where possible; a single peak season
  T-12 overstates.
- Management is heavy: 12–15% equivalent if owner-operated.
- Verify: septic/well capacity, utility metering, transient zoning permission.

## Mobile home parks

- **Lot rent only is the institutional income.** Park-owned-home (POH) rent is a
  separate, deteriorating income stem — underwrite POH income at heavy discount or
  zero, and POH maintenance realistically (it eats 30–50% of POH rent).
- Vacancy on lots: physical AND economic (non-paying residents in place).
- Infrastructure is the deal: private utilities (well/septic/private lines) demand
  price concessions and inspection reserves.
- Expense ratio floor: 30–40% of lot-rent EGI for stable parks (higher w/ POH, private
  utilities).

## Self-storage

- **Economic occupancy ≠ physical occupancy.** Street-rate discounting, promos, and
  delinquency mean economic occupancy typically runs 8–15 points below physical. Use
  economic.
- Normalize revenue on achieved rent per occupied sq ft, not advertised rates.
- Expense ratio: 28–38% typical for single-site; add third-party management 6% + payroll
  if unmanned claims look optimistic.
- Watch: new supply within 3–5 mile trade area (it resets street rates market-wide).

## Small commercial (office / retail / industrial)

- Lease-by-lease, not blended: term, escalations, options, recovery structure
  (NNN vs. gross vs. modified) per tenant.
- Vacancy between leases: months of downtime + TI + leasing commissions on rollover —
  model per lease expiry, not a flat %.
- Credit: mom-and-pop tenant at above-market rent is a liability dressed as income.

### Mixed recovery structures — where a center's P&L stops being true

*** THE MOST COMMON REAL CASE IS NOT A CLEAN NNN BUILDING. *** It is a center where
SOME tenants are NNN, a few are gross or modified gross, and the common-area costs are
pooled and billed pro-rata across the net tenants only. The P&L then shows CAM
reimbursement as income and CAM as expense, and **they do not net to zero**. The gap is
real money the landlord pays, every year, and it is invisible in a cap rate computed off
a summary NOI. Do not accept a blended expense ratio on a center with mixed leases — the
recovery structure IS the deal.

**Compute the recovery ratio and put it on the page.** Total reimbursement income ÷
total recoverable expense, from the ACTUALS, not the pro forma. If it comes out at 78%,
that is the truth of this center and the missing 22% is the landlord's. A broker's
package will routinely show reimbursements grossed up to 100% while showing expenses at
actual — that single inconsistency can move NOI by more than the whole value-add thesis.

**Then find out WHY it is not 100%**, because each cause behaves differently going
forward:

- **The vacancy gap.** Vacant space has no tenant to bill, so the landlord eats its
  share. How big that bite is depends on a lease detail worth real money: whether each
  tenant's pro-rata share is computed on **occupied** GLA or **total** GLA. On occupied
  GLA the remaining tenants absorb the vacancy; on total GLA the landlord does. Read it;
  do not assume. Model the recovery ratio at the center's realistic occupancy, not at
  today's.
- **Gross and modified-gross tenants.** They pay no CAM, or none above a base year, so
  their share is structurally landlord expense for as long as those leases run. A center
  with three gross tenants can never reach full recovery, and no amount of management
  fixes it before those leases expire. Note the expiry — that is when the recovery ratio
  can actually change.
- **Base years and expense stops.** On modified gross, the landlord carries everything
  up to the base-year figure and only bills growth above it. A base year set in a
  low-expense year is a quiet permanent cost.
- **CAM caps.** Many leases cap increases on CONTROLLABLE expenses — often around 5% a
  year, sometimes cumulative and sometimes not, and the cumulative form is far more
  landlord-friendly. Once actual costs run past the cap, the excess is the landlord's.
  Snow, security and landscaping are where this bites.
- **The admin fee.** CAM is usually billed with an administrative or management load on
  top, commonly 10–15%. Whether it is recoverable, and whether it applies to taxes and
  insurance too, varies lease by lease and is frequently assumed rather than verified.

**Taxes and insurance are usually recovered separately from CAM, and the reassessment is
the classic killer.** A sale triggers reassessment in most jurisdictions; taxes jump; and
whether the landlord can pass that jump through depends on each lease's base year, cap
and pass-through language. Underwrite the reassessed figure, not the seller's, and then
check tenant by tenant how much of the increase is actually recoverable. A center that
pencils on the seller's tax bill and not on yours is not a deal.

**Anchor versus inline is a different negotiation, not a bigger one.** Anchors commonly
carry their own CAM arrangement — a fixed contribution, a capped number, or self-performed
maintenance — so they do not scale with the pool the way inline tenants do. And read the
**co-tenancy clauses**: inline leases frequently let tenants cut to percentage rent or
terminate outright if the anchor goes dark or occupancy falls below a threshold. That
turns one anchor departure into a cascade, and it is the shopping-center-specific risk
that a rent roll alone will never show.

**Percentage rent, where it exists, is not base income.** Cap it separately and at a
discount, and get the tenant's sales history if you can — rent-to-sales tells you whether
that tenant renews. A tenant paying an unsustainable share of sales in rent is a vacancy
with a date on it.

### Single-tenant net lease — a different instrument entirely

When there is ONE tenant on a long net lease, you are buying credit and term more than
real estate, and the cap rate is set by the guarantor and the years remaining rather than
by the local market. The rules above barely apply; these do:

- **Who actually signs.** Corporate guarantee, franchisee entity, and single-store LLC are
  three very different risks that trade at deceptively similar cap rates. The sign over
  the door is not the guarantor — find the entity and what stands behind it.
- **What "NNN" means in THIS lease.** The term is used loosely. Absolute net puts roof and
  structure on the tenant; "NN" leaves them with the landlord. On an older building that
  is a six-figure difference that never appears in the going-in yield.
- **Term remaining against the debt.** A lease with six years left behind a loan that
  balloons in seven means refinancing a building that may be empty. Compare the two
  explicitly and say what happens if they cross.
- **Escalations against inflation.** Flat rent for a decade, or 10% every five years
  (about 1.9% a year), is a real-terms decline that a year-one cap rate hides completely.
- **Dark value — underwrite the building empty.** What is it worth with no tenant? A
  build-to-suit rural store, a bank branch with a vault and drive-through, a purpose-built
  daycare: near-zero alternative use. Price the re-tenanting cost, the downtime, and the
  rent a second-generation tenant would really pay, which is usually well below the
  original. **That number, not the going-in cap rate, is the downside of the deal.**
- **Options belong to the tenant.** A renewal option at below-market rent only ever gets
  exercised against the landlord; never model option years as upside.

## SFR portfolios

- Underwrite per-door, then portfolio: scattered-site management runs 8–12% + leasing
  fees; maintenance per door exceeds multifamily equivalents (roofs, HVACs are not
  shared).
- Tape review: rent roll vs. actual collections per door; watch for vacant units
  listed at market rent.
- Exit is dual: retail one-by-one (higher gross, long tail, holding costs) vs. bulk
  (discount 10–20%). Model both.

## Land

Land is the one class here with **no income to normalize**. Step 2 has nothing to
rebuild, and Steps 3, 6 and 7 do not apply as written. Do not screen a land deal on the
cap-rate standard: there is no NOI to measure a cap rate against, so the row can neither
pass nor fail — it just sits in the queue forever waiting on a T-12 that will never exist
for a vacant parcel. Land gets the rules below instead.

### First, name the play — it decides everything after it

Land money comes from six places. They differ most in **how long you actually own the
parcel**, which is what decides whether carry matters at all. Name the play before
touching a number, and say which one in the output.

**A. Wholesale / assignment / double close — you barely own it.** Put it under contract,
assign the contract or close and resell the same day, and collect a fee or a spread.
Days to a few weeks. **Carry is effectively zero, so do NOT screen this on a
carry-loaded margin** — there is no hold to load. It is screened on the spread: what you
can contract it for as a percentage of market, and the dollars left after closing costs
both ways and the double-close funding cost if there is one. The risk here is not the
hold, it is the **buyer**: no end buyer, no deal, and an assignment that does not close
costs the earnest money. So underwrite the buyer list and the inspection/assignment
window, not the carrying cost. Land wholesale is a real and common business — this is
usually what someone means when they say they flip land.

**B. Resale flip — you own it briefly.** Buy under market, hold thirty days to a year,
sell as-is to a retail buyer, a neighbor or a builder. Real carry, but small. Value
anchor is $/usable acre from comps.

**C. Owner-financed resale — you sell it on terms.** The same buy as B, but the exit is a
**note, not cash**: a small down payment and monthly payments over years. This changes
what is being underwritten. The yield is on the paper, not on the dirt, so measure
yield-to-maturity AND yield-to-default-recovery, and read the **Notes / seller-financed
paper** section below for the paper side — payer quality, servicing, and what
foreclosure or forfeiture actually costs and takes in that state. The land comes back on
default, which is sometimes the real business model; say so plainly if it is, and
underwrite the re-sale cost of getting it back and selling it again.

**D. Entitlement — you change what it legally is.** Rezone, variance, lot split, plat,
utility extension, then sell entitled. **The approval IS the deal.** If it does not come,
the buyer owns the "before" parcel at the "after" price. Underwrite the denial, not just
the approval. Long hold, so carry dominates.

**E. Development residual — what a builder can actually pay.** Work backwards: finished
value − hard cost − soft cost − developer profit − carry = residual land value. The land
is what is LEFT OVER, never the starting point. A seller's asking price is an opinion;
the residual is the answer.

**F. Income land — it pays you while you hold it.** Ag or crop lease, hunting lease,
timber, billboard, cell-tower ground lease, solar or wind option, laydown yard. This one
has durable contractual income: underwrite it on the owner's cap-rate standard like any
other income asset, with the dirt as the residual underneath. This is the only land play
that uses the cap-rate branch, and it uses it properly.

**Which standard screens which play:**

- **A (wholesale)** — `land_max_pct_of_market` and `land_min_spread`. A percentage margin
  over all-in is the wrong instrument when all-in is a three-week option on someone
  else's land.
- **B, D, E (owned plays)** — `land_margin_target` / `land_margin_floor`, measured on
  all-in cost INCLUDING CARRY **over the hold this deal actually requires**, checked
  against `land_max_carry_months`. A thirty-day flip carries thirty days, not
  twenty-four months; do not load a long-hold assumption onto a short deal, and do not
  let a short-hold assumption rescue an entitlement play that will take three years.
- **C (owner-financed)** — the margin standard on the sale itself, then the paper on its
  own yield. Both, separately. A strong headline price on weak paper is not a strong deal.
- **F (income land)** — the cap-rate standard.

A parcel can carry two plays at once — an ag lease during a five-year entitlement hold.
When it does, underwrite the lease as income and the exit as entitlement, and never let
the lease income rescue a margin the exit does not support.

### The Land Kill Filter — legal and physical facts, before comps

Same principle as the fix-and-flip Kill Filter: kill losers cheap. But for land the
killers are **binary, not numeric**. A parcel that fails any one of these is worth a
fraction of comp value no matter what $/acre says, and every one of them is answerable
from a county GIS site, a plat, or the auditor's card — minutes of desk work, before
spending any time on comps.

- **LEGAL ACCESS.** Does a deeded, recorded right of access reach a public road? "There's
  a gravel drive" is not access; a neighbor's tolerance is not access. A landlocked parcel
  with only a prescriptive or implied claim is worth a fraction of comp value until an
  easement is recorded, and the neighbor who must grant it knows exactly what it is worth.
  This is the single most common land killer and the cheapest one to check.
- **PERC AND SOILS**, wherever there is no public sewer. No septic approval means no
  house, which means no retail buyer. An expired or failed perc is a NO until re-tested,
  and test season is weather-dependent — a parcel can be unperc-able for months of the
  year, which is a timeline risk on top of a value risk.
- **UTILITIES — at the parcel, not "in the area."** Public water, sewer, electric and gas
  each either reach the line or do not. Get the distance and who pays for the extension;
  a run measured in hundreds of feet is a real five-figure number and a run measured in
  thousands can exceed the price of the land.
- **FLOODPLAIN AND WETLANDS.** FEMA flood zone from the map, and wetlands from the
  National Wetlands Inventory as a screen — the NWI is indicative only, and a delineation
  is what actually binds. Wetlands are the quiet one: they do not stop the purchase, they
  stop the building, and they are often invisible on a dry-season walk.
- **ZONING AND PERMITTED USE**, as it is today, not as the listing describes it. Also the
  minimum lot size, setbacks, frontage requirement and any overlay — those are what decide
  how many lots the acreage actually yields, which is usually a smaller number than
  acreage ÷ minimum lot size.
- **BOUNDARY AND SURVEY.** Deeded acreage is frequently not surveyed acreage, especially
  on older metes-and-bounds descriptions. Encroachments, old fence lines and unrecorded
  splits surface here. Price the survey in; on anything irregular, make it a contingency.
- **SEVERED RIGHTS.** Mineral, oil and gas, timber and water rights can be severed from
  the surface and often are. A severed mineral owner may hold the right to enter and
  develop the surface — which can end a residential exit outright.
- **BACK TAXES, LIENS AND ASSESSMENTS.** Delinquent land is common and often the reason it
  is cheap; special assessments for a road or sewer district ride with the parcel.

Run these first. Report each as answered, unresolved, or a kill — and **name the
unresolved ones in the verdict**, because on land the unknowns, not the knowns, are what
determine the price.

### Comps: usable acres, not deeded acres

Value the land on the acreage that can actually be used — deeded acres minus floodplain,
wetlands, right-of-way, steep or unbuildable topography, and anything under a severed
right. A hundred-acre parcel with sixty usable acres is a sixty-acre parcel with a large
tax bill. Quote $/usable acre, state the deeded figure alongside it, and say how the
usable number was derived.

Two other adjustments matter more on land than anywhere else: **parcel size runs inverse
to price per acre** (five acres trades well above the per-acre price of a hundred, so
never comp across size classes without adjusting), and **frontage and shape** carry real
value where road frontage is what permits a split.

### Carry is the expense model

There is no NOI, so on every play where you OWN the parcel the expense side is entirely
carrying cost, and it is what kills patient land deals. (On play A it is close to nothing
— a wholesale deal is priced on the spread, not the hold, so do not manufacture a carry
number for it.) Model it monthly and total it over the realistic hold: property taxes,
insurance, mowing or brush-hogging where a municipality requires it, association or
district assessments, and interest if financed. Then state **months of carry at this
burn** as a headline figure next to the margin.

**Ag-valuation recoupment.** Land taxed under an agricultural or current-use program
(CAUV in Ohio, "rollback taxes" in Texas and Virginia, the Williamson Act in California —
almost every state has one) carries a clawback when the use changes or the program is
withdrawn: several years of the tax savings become due, usually at closing or on the
change of use. It is a five-figure surprise on a modest parcel and it is invisible on the
listing. Check the auditor's card for the current-use flag and name the recoupment
exposure explicitly, or say plainly that it could not be determined.

**The carry-capacity test replaces the refi test** on the owned plays. There is no NOI to
stress and nothing to refinance, so Step 7 becomes: can the buyer carry this parcel for
the realistic hold × 1.5 without selling something else? On a wholesale deal the
equivalent stress is different and should be run instead: **what happens if the end buyer
walks** — how much earnest money is at risk, how long the inspection window runs, whether
the contract is assignable at all, and whether there is a second buyer. Say the number of months, the monthly burn, and where
the money comes from. A land deal that only works if it sells on schedule is a timing bet,
and it should be labelled one.

### Classification and verdict

Classify as **Speculative** unless it is entitled with a defined exit — a signed builder
contract, a recorded plat with lot sales, an option from a credible counterparty. An
entitlement play is Speculative by definition until the approval is in hand, however
likely it looks; say what the parcel is worth if the approval never comes, and put that
number in the verdict.

## Hospitality — hotels and motels

There are no leases and there is no rent roll. Revenue re-prices every night, so a
twelve-month history is a starting point and never a forecast.

- **The three numbers, in this order:** occupancy, ADR (average daily rate), and RevPAR
  (revenue per available room = occupancy × ADR). RevPAR is the one that matters, because
  a property can buy occupancy by cutting rate and show growth while earning less.
- **Compare to the competitive set**, not to itself. A STR report showing index against
  the comp set tells you whether management or the market is the problem; without one,
  say you could not tell them apart.
- **Expenses run 65–75% of revenue**, not the 35–45% of an apartment building. Departmental
  costs, labour, franchise fees, marketing and reservation fees, and an FF&E reserve of
  4–5% of revenue that is NOT optional — the brand will require it.
- **The franchise agreement is a liability as much as an asset.** Term remaining,
  transfer fee and approval, and above all the PIP: a property improvement plan the brand
  can demand on transfer, routinely a six- or seven-figure obligation landing in year one.
  Get the PIP estimate in writing before pricing anything.
- **Management is a contract, not an assumption.** Term, fee, incentive, termination
  rights. A brand-mandated manager you cannot fire is a constraint on every other number.
- **Seasonality and demand concentration.** One employer, one event, one highway
  interchange, one construction crew filling rooms for eighteen months. Ask what would
  happen if it left, and underwrite that.
- **Dark value is low.** A purpose-built motel has almost no alternative use short of
  conversion, and conversion is a development project, not a fallback.

## Marinas

Several income streams with different durability, plus infrastructure that is expensive
and under water.

- **Separate the stems:** annual slip rentals (the durable income, closest to rent),
  transient dockage (seasonal and volatile), dry stack and trailer storage, fuel, service
  and repair, ship's store, restaurant. Cap the durable stem; treat fuel and service as
  operating margin, never revenue.
- **Fuel is margin, and thin.** Gallons times spread, not gallons times price, and it
  carries environmental exposure most buyers underprice.
- **Slip count is not capacity.** Vessel length mix decides revenue — the same water sells
  very differently at 25 feet than at 50. Get the mix and the waiting list.
- **Submerged land is frequently leased, not owned**, from a state or port authority, with
  its own term, rent, reversion and transfer consent. Treat it exactly like a ground lease
  and run the Leasehold tab.
- **The infrastructure is the deal:** dock and piling condition and remaining life, seawall
  and bulkhead, dredging obligations and permitted depth, electrical service to the docks.
  Dredging and seawall work are capital events big enough to wipe out several years of NOI.
- **Permits and environment:** fuel dock and tank compliance, pump-out, stormwater,
  wetlands, and any consent order. Ask directly whether there has ever been a spill.

## Senior housing and assisted living

A licensed healthcare operation with real estate attached. Independent living is closer to
apartments; assisted living and memory care are not.

- **Revenue is per resident per day**, by care level, so census and acuity mix both drive
  it. A facility can hold census while revenue falls if acuity drops.
- **Labour is the business.** Staffing ratios are set by licence, agency labour is the
  margin killer, and turnover is a real operating metric. Expense ratios run high and are
  not negotiable downward without licence consequences.
- **The licence is the asset and it may not transfer.** Change of ownership approval,
  survey history, deficiencies and any plan of correction. A facility with an open
  enforcement action is not a normal purchase.
- **Payor mix:** private pay versus Medicaid or other programme reimbursement, which
  changes both rate and collection risk, and which can be changed by policy rather than by
  anything you do.
- **Ask whether the operator conveys.** Buying the building with the operator staying is a
  different deal from buying the operation, and this class is the most common place that
  question gets skipped.

## Medical office

Looks like office and behaves differently.

- **Tenant improvement costs are far higher** than general office — plumbing, gas, lead
  shielding, specialised power — so rollover is expensive and tenants are correspondingly
  sticky.
- **Location relative to the hospital campus** drives rent more than the submarket does.
  On-campus, adjacent and unaffiliated are three different rent tiers.
- **Health system affiliation is the credit question.** A practice backed by a system is a
  different covenant from an independent group, and consolidation can empty a building
  when a system relocates its physicians.
- **Check reimbursement exposure by specialty** — a tenant whose procedure mix depends on
  one reimbursement rate is a single-payor risk wearing a lease.

## Student housing

Apartments on an academic calendar, with a different leasing mechanic.

- **Leased by the bed, not by the unit**, usually twelve-month terms with parental
  guarantees. Occupancy is measured on beds.
- **Pre-leasing velocity is the forward indicator** — the percentage leased at a given
  week before the academic year, against the same week last year. It is the only early
  warning this asset gives you.
- **Turn is total and it happens in one week.** Budget turn cost per bed accordingly; it
  dwarfs conventional multifamily.
- **Distance to campus and the shuttle route** decide rent, and a university's own new
  housing can reset the market in a single year. Check enrolment trend and on-campus
  housing plans before anything else.

## Car washes

An operating business almost always, real estate sometimes, and currently a hot enough
sector that pricing has run ahead of fundamentals in places.

- **Express tunnel, in-bay automatic and self-serve are three different businesses** with
  different volumes, labour and capital.
- **Membership is the whole thesis.** Monthly plan count, churn rate and capture rate
  matter more than car count — recurring revenue is what the multiples are being paid for.
  Get member counts by month for three years, not an average.
- **Cars per day against site capacity**, plus the traffic count and the competitor within
  a mile that opened last year.
- **Equipment age and remaining life**, water reclamation, and the sewer discharge permit.
- **Value it as a business**, and separate the dirt with the market-rent test above. A high
  multiple on earnings that include free rent is the classic overpay in this sector.

## Industrial outdoor storage (IOS)

Truck, trailer and container yards — close to land with a use.

- **Income is per acre**, not per square foot of building. The improvements are fencing,
  lighting, drainage, surface and gate.
- **The entitlement is the asset.** Outdoor storage is often grandfathered or specially
  permitted and cannot be replicated nearby, which is the whole scarcity argument. Confirm
  the use is legal and transferable, not merely long-standing.
- **Surface condition is a real capital item** — millings, asphalt or concrete carry very
  different maintenance and support different loads.
- **Short leases, month-to-month tenancy and credit that is thinner than the rent roll
  suggests.** Underwrite rollover accordingly.
- Where the site is unentitled or the play is to get it approved, use the **Land** tab and
  its entitlement play instead.

## Mixed-use

Residential over retail behaves like neither parent and should never be blended.

- **Underwrite each component separately, then combine.** Residential on apartment rules,
  ground-floor retail on the commercial rules including its recovery structure, and any
  office on its own.
- **The retail carries the risk and the residential carries the stability.** A vacant
  ground floor on a full building still guts the NOI and it is far harder to re-let than
  the apartments above it.
- **Financing is where mixed-use trips people.** Lenders apply a commercial-income
  percentage test; cross that threshold and residential-style terms disappear. Find the
  threshold before you assume the debt.
- **Shared systems and allocation** — one boiler, one roof, one water service across two
  uses — decide what is recoverable from whom. Read the leases.
- **Residential tenant protections apply to the residential portion** regardless of the
  commercial wrapper, including notice periods and any local rent regulation.
